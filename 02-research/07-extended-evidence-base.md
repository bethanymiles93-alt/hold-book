# Extended evidence base — communication, connection and behaviour change

**Logged 2026-08-11.** A larger research batch compiled in one session, covering social connection, weak ties, reach-out and disclosure psychology, habit formation, loneliness interventions, plan-making, chronic-illness-specific findings, shame/guilt, and colour-mood research. Organised by theme, matching the groupings the sources were originally compiled under. Each finding is tagged **[Causal]**, **[Correlational]**, **[Descriptive/qualitative]**, **[Theoretical]**, or **[Meta-analysis, mixed designs]** — see "A note on the causal/correlational tags" below for what each means and why the distinction matters here specifically.

This sits alongside, not instead of, the existing evidence already logged in `02-research/06-guilt-spiral-and-supportive-language.md` (lived-experience/support-guidance sources) and `05-relationship-continuity-landscape.md` (competitor/category analysis) — those remain the sources for the claims they already support.

## A note on the causal/correlational tags

Most of what's below is observational: it tells us two things are associated, not that one causes the other. That matters for how confidently Hold's own copy or design reasoning can lean on a finding. A **[Causal]** tag means the study used random assignment or a controlled intervention, so a cause-and-effect claim is reasonably well supported. A **[Correlational]** tag means the finding is a real, often large, statistical association from observational or longitudinal data, but alternative explanations (reverse causation, a shared underlying cause) haven't been ruled out. **[Descriptive/qualitative]** means the finding describes what people report or experience, often from a small sample, without a statistical causal or correlational claim being made at all. **[Theoretical]** means the source is a conceptual framework rather than a single empirical result. Nothing below should be treated as stronger evidence than its tag indicates.

## Social connection / mortality

- Holt-Lunstad, Smith & Layton (2010), *PLoS Medicine*, DOI: 10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316 — meta-analysis finding social relationships influence mortality risk to a similar magnitude as other established risk factors, including smoking. **[Correlational — meta-analysis of prospective observational studies; social relationships cannot be randomly assigned, so this is association, not proof of a causal mechanism, however large and consistent.]**
- Holt-Lunstad, Smith, Baker, Harris & Stephenson (2015), *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, DOI: 10.1177/1745691614568352 — social isolation OR = 1.29, loneliness OR = 1.26, living alone OR = 1.32 (~29%, 26%, 32% increased mortality likelihood respectively). **[Correlational — same reasoning as above.]**

**Relevance to Hold:** the strongest available evidence that maintaining social connection during a hard period isn't a "nice to have" — it's tied to outcomes on the same order as other major health risk factors. Supports Hold's core premise, not any specific design choice.

## Weak ties

- Sandstrom & Dunn (2014), *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*, DOI: 10.1177/0146167214529799 — "weak tie" (acquaintance-level) interactions linked to greater daily belonging and wellbeing; effect present even controlling for strong-tie interactions. **[Correlational — naturalistic daily-diary study, no manipulation.]**
- Sandstrom & Dunn (2013/2014), *Social Psychological and Personality Science*, DOI: 10.1177/1948550613502990 — brief, genuine interactions with strangers (e.g. a barista) produced more positive affect than maximally efficient/minimal exchanges. **[Causal — field experiment with random assignment to interaction style.]**
- Moreton et al. (2023), *Social and Personality Psychology Compass* — weak ties account for roughly 60% of people's daily social interactions in tracked-diary studies. **[Descriptive — a measured proportion from diary data, not a causal or correlational claim.]**

**Relevance to Hold:** supports treating Friends/Work-tier Circles as genuinely valuable to maintain, not just a lesser version of Close Circle contact — consistent with the content-depth-tiering guidance added to `04-ux-content/02-voice-and-language.md` (2026-08-11), which varies depth by Circle type without implying outer circles matter less.

## Reach-out underestimation / liking gap / compliance underestimation

- Liu, Rim & Min (2022), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, DOI: 10.1037/pspi0000402 — across ~5,900 participants and 13 preregistered experiments, people robustly underestimate how much others appreciate being reached out to; appreciation is greater the more surprising the reach-out, and the effect is stronger for weak ties. **[Causal — preregistered experiments comparing predicted vs. actual appreciation.]**
- Boothby, Cooney, Sandstrom & Clark (2018), *Psychological Science*, DOI: 10.1177/0956797618783714 — "the liking gap": after conversations, people systematically underestimate how much their conversation partner liked them and enjoyed the interaction, observed across strangers, new dormmates, and workshop attendees. **[Correlational/descriptive — naturalistic conversations followed by paired self/partner ratings, not an experimental manipulation of the gap itself.]**
- Bohns (2016), *Current Directions in Psychological Science*, DOI: 10.1177/0963721415628011 — the "underestimation-of-compliance effect": across studies involving 14,000+ requests to strangers, people underestimate the likelihood others will agree to a direct request for help by an average of 48%, because requesters fail to appreciate how awkward it is for someone to say no. **[Causal/descriptive hybrid — compares real-world request outcomes to pre-registered predictions; the underlying compliance data is observational, but the prediction-vs-outcome gap is a directly measured effect.]**

**Relevance to Hold:** directly supports the core hypothesis behind Reconnect and reach-out prompts — the person going quiet is likely to underestimate both how welcome a message would be and how much people already like them, which is exactly the gap Hold's reassurance copy is trying to close.

## Habit formation

- Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts & Wardle (2010), *European Journal of Social Psychology*, DOI: 10.1002/ejsp.674 — real-world habit formation study; time to reach 95% of automaticity ranged from 18–254 days (median often cited as 66, but with huge individual variation). Critically: missing a single opportunity did not materially disrupt the habit formation process. **[Correlational — longitudinal self-report tracking, no manipulation of habit-formation strategy.]**

**Relevance to Hold:** the "missing a day doesn't break it" finding is directly relevant to Hold's no-streaks, no-guilt design principle already in `01-foundation/03-principles.md` — this is evidence for a decision already made, not a new one.

## Loneliness interventions

- Lindsay, Young, Brown, Smyth & Creswell (2019), *PNAS*, DOI: 10.1073/pnas.1813588116 — RCT: 2-week smartphone mindfulness training (monitoring + acceptance) reduced daily loneliness by 22% and increased social contact by ~2 interactions/day vs. active control. **[Causal — randomised controlled trial.]**
- Masi, Chen, Hawkley & Cacioppo (2011), *Personality and Social Psychology Review*, DOI: 10.1177/1088868310377394 — meta-analysis of loneliness interventions across 4 strategies (social skills, social support, opportunities for contact, maladaptive cognition). Key finding: interventions addressing maladaptive social cognition had the greatest average effectiveness — not simply increasing opportunities for contact (though based on limited RCTs, flagged by the authors themselves as needing replication). **[Meta-analysis, mixed designs — synthesises studies of varying rigour; the authors' own caveat about limited RCTs for the strongest-performing category is carried forward here, not smoothed over.]**
- Lasgaard et al. — a more recent meta-analysis of 128 studies found psychological treatment, social support, and social/emotional skills training the most effective strategies — broadly consistent with the above. **[Meta-analysis, mixed designs — year/DOI not provided in the source compilation; treat as consistent with, not independent confirmation of, Masi et al. above until a full citation is available.]**

**Relevance to Hold:** cautions against assuming "more opportunities to contact people" (e.g. more nudges, more prompts) is automatically the highest-leverage intervention — addressing the cognitive/emotional loop around reaching out (which is closer to what Hold's reassurance and permission-giving copy already does) has stronger evidence behind it.

## Plan-making / Zeigarnik effect / implementation intentions

- Masicampo & Baumeister (2011), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, DOI: 10.1037/a0024192 — unfinished goals cause intrusive thoughts and reduced performance on unrelated tasks; forming a specific plan eliminated this effect, but only when the plan was genuine enough to be later executed — a vague or unwritten "plan" (e.g. drafting a reply mentally) does not provide real closure. **[Causal — controlled lab experiments.]**
- Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* — meta-analysis of 94 tests: "if-then" implementation intentions (specific when/where/how plans) had a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment (d = 0.65), particularly effective at promoting initiation of action. Later replicated across 642 tests. **[Meta-analysis of causal (experimental) studies — implementation intentions were manipulated, not just observed.]**

**Relevance to Hold:** supports the value of Hold's structured, specific flows (a real plan: who, roughly what, via which route) over an open-ended "I'll reach out at some point" intention — and is a caution that a half-finished draft saved without being sent may not provide the closure a fully "done" action would.

## Perfectionism / procrastination in chronic illness

- Sirois, F. M. (2014). Procrastination and stress: Exploring the role of self-compassion. *Self and Identity*, 13(2), 128–145. — guilt/self-criticism is what maintains procrastination, not what interrupts it; low self-compassion predicts more procrastination and more stress from it. **[Correlational — survey-based study relating self-compassion, procrastination and stress.]**
- Sirois, F. M., Molnar, D. S., & Hirsch, J. K. (2015). Self-compassion, stress and coping in the context of chronic illness. *Self and Identity*, 14, 334–347. — extends the same self-compassion/stress-coping relationship specifically to a chronic-illness population. **[Correlational — survey-based study.]**

**Citation note (resolved 2026-08-11):** both papers apply and are cited together, deliberately, not as an either/or judgement call — Hold's content draws on both the procrastination-avoidance mechanism (2014) and the chronic-illness-specific framing (2015). Previously flagged as missing full citation detail; now resolved via direct web search against the real papers, not reconstructed from memory.

**Relevance to Hold:** supports "never imply failure" and the broader no-guilt voice rules in `04-ux-content/02-voice-and-language.md` — guilt-based framing is the thing this literature says keeps people stuck, not the thing that motivates them to act.

## Active vs. passive social media use

- Verduyn et al. (2017, 2022 extended model), *Current Directions in Psychological Science* — active use (direct messages, comments, genuine exchange) linked to increased social capital/connectedness; passive use (scrolling, browsing) linked to upward social comparison and envy, and reduced wellbeing. **[Correlational, with some experimental components in the broader literature this model synthesises — treat the active/passive distinction itself as well-supported, the causal mechanism as still being refined.]**

**Relevance to Hold:** supports Hold's decision (see `08-decisions/04-open-questions.md`, the community photo-sharing out-of-scope note) to avoid building any passive-scroll/feed-style surface — this model is a further piece of evidence for that instinct, not the original reason for it.

## Time perception in chronic pain

- Ogden et al. (2024), *European Journal of Pain*, DOI: 10.1002/ejp.2211 — mixed-methods study: chronic pain patients associate distortions in their sense of time passing with increased negative affect and greater impairment. Notably situation-specific — not reliably tied to depression/anxiety scores in the quantitative data, though patients' own accounts linked it to worse affect. **[Correlational (quantitative component) + descriptive/qualitative (patient accounts) — the study itself flags that the quantitative and qualitative findings don't fully align, which is preserved here rather than smoothed into one clean claim.]**

**Relevance to Hold:** supports allowing "I don't know" and no fixed end date for a quiet period (already in `02-research/02-low-capacity-design.md`'s recommendations) — time can genuinely feel distorted during the exact periods Hold is designed for.

## Self-perceived burden

- Wilson, Kowal, Caird, Castillo, McWilliams & Heenan, *Canadian Journal of Pain* (2017) — established construct in chronic pain/illness research; belief that one is "a burden to others" is a recognized, studied source of guilt and distress. **[Correlational — construct-validation and associational research, not an experimental manipulation of burden beliefs.]**

**Relevance to Hold:** names the specific fear ("I'm a burden") that Hold's reassurance copy is designed to counter, distinct from general guilt about not replying.

## Friendship navigation in chronic illness

- Moensted et al. (2023), *SSM – Qualitative Research in Health* — 40 interviews with chronically ill people experiencing loneliness; maintaining friendship required significant "personal labour" and negotiation between concealment and authenticity to preserve social dignity. **[Descriptive/qualitative — interview-based, not a statistical claim.]**

**Relevance to Hold:** the "personal labour" of managing what to disclose is exactly the labour Hold is trying to reduce at the point of going quiet or reconnecting.

## Network bridging/narrowing

- Cornwell (2023), *Social Networks*, NSHAP longitudinal data (N=1,555) — chronic illness associated with lower network "bridging," more frequent contact with close ties, fewer neighbours/friends/colleagues. **[Correlational — longitudinal survey data.]**
- Longitudinal Aging Study Amsterdam (LASA), N=2,788 — network size not associated with disease status, but loneliness and instrumental support were — illness narrows the outer layer more than it shrinks the network overall. **[Correlational — longitudinal survey data.]**

**Relevance to Hold:** supports designing for multiple Circles of differing closeness (Close/Friends/Work) rather than treating "your network" as one undifferentiated group — the outer layers are specifically what's at risk during a hard period, which is exactly what Hold's per-Circle messaging addresses.

## Reciprocity / equity theory

- Reciprocity review (PubMed 20871667) — family relationships tolerate imbalance better over time than friendships; friendship continuity depends more on maintained, balanced reciprocity. **[Correlational/theoretical review — full journal citation not provided beyond the PMID.]**

**Relevance to Hold:** a possible reason Friends-tier relationships may need more active maintenance during a quiet period than family ties do — consistent with, though not the origin of, the Close/Friends/Work content-depth guidance.

## Disclosure decision-making

- Joachim & Acorn (2000), *Journal of Advanced Nursing* — foundational framework linking a chronic condition's visibility to the ongoing decision to disclose or conceal. **[Theoretical — conceptual framework, not a single statistical finding.]**
- Disclosure Decision-Making Model (Chaudoir & Fisher; applied to nonvisible illness, PMC5215027) — perceived stigma negatively predicts disclosure efficacy; closeness to the recipient positively predicts both disclosure efficacy and anticipated positive response. **[Correlational — model tested via self-report path analysis, not experimental manipulation.]**

**Relevance to Hold:** this is the direct evidentiary basis for the content-depth-by-Circle-type guidance already added to `04-ux-content/02-voice-and-language.md` (2026-08-11) — closeness predicting disclosure comfort and positive reception is the specific finding that guidance operationalises.

## AI-assisted writing authenticity

- Coman & Cardon (2026) — sincerity/caring ratings measurably dip when text reads as AI-generated. **[Causal — described as a rating-based experiment comparing perceived-AI vs. perceived-human text; DOI not provided in the source compilation.]**

**Relevance to Hold:** relevant context for Amend with AI's design as a "light-touch blend, not a from-scratch regenerate" (`07-business/02-pricing-principles.md`) — if AI-generated text reads as less sincere, keeping the user's own words as the base rather than fully regenerating supports the sincerity the recipient will actually perceive.

## Dunbar's layered relationships

- Dunbar's layered relationship model (Dunbar, 1992 onward; primary paper: 2025, *PLOS One*, DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0319604) — personal networks nest in layers: an innermost "support clique" (~5) and a "sympathy group" (~10 more, "good friends, close but not closest"), with the two inner layers together forming the "core network." **[Descriptive/structural — based on observed network-size distributions across studies, not a causal claim.]**

**Relevance to Hold:** a structural rationale for Circles being small, named groups rather than one undifferentiated contact list — consistent with, though not the origin of, Hold's existing Close/Friends/Work Circle model.

## Patient activation

- Hibbard, J. H., Stockard, J., Mahoney, E. R., & Tusler, M. (2004). Development of the Patient Activation Measure (PAM): Conceptualizing and measuring activation in patients and consumers. *Health Services Research*, 39(4 Pt 1), 1005–1026. DOI: 10.1111/j.1475-6773.2004.00269.x — the foundational, most-cited paper defining and measuring "patient activation" (a person's knowledge, skill and confidence in managing their own health). **[Correlational/psychometric — scale-development and validation study, not an experimental manipulation of activation itself.]** **Citation resolved 2026-08-11** via direct web search; this is the field's foundational paper, high confidence.

## Shame vs. guilt

- Tangney et al. — shame is a negative evaluation of the self as a whole; guilt is a negative appraisal of a specific behaviour, not the self. Guilt tends to motivate reparative behaviour; shame tends to motivate withdrawal. Shame-prone people attribute failures to fixed, global character traits; guilt-prone people attribute failures to specific, fixable behaviour. **[Correlational/theoretical — a well-established framework built on attributional and self-report research; not one of the three entries the source instruction flagged as needing a citation check, but noted here for completeness that a specific paper/year/DOI wasn't given either.]**

Already applied: this is the direct basis for the "Shame and guilt are not the same thing" section added to `02-research/06-guilt-spiral-and-supportive-language.md` on 2026-08-11, and the identity-vs-behaviour voice rule added there.

## The shame spiral

- Coined by Gershen Kaufman, *Journal of Counseling Psychology*, 1974 — a triggering event leads to shame feelings that "flow in a circle, endlessly triggering each other." Real academic origin; most further elaboration available today is wellness-blog content, not peer-reviewed, so treat detailed staged models as illustrative rather than clinically validated. **[Theoretical — original conceptual paper; later popular elaborations explicitly not peer-reviewed, flagged as such by the source itself.]**

## ADHD / Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria

- Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria (RSD) — not a formal DSM diagnosis, but a growing, real research area (Dodson et al., 2024 case studies; Sandland, 2025 qualitative study of 7 neurodivergent adults). Described as episodic, intense shame and feeling ostracised triggered by perceived or actual rejection/criticism, frequently leading to self-silencing or avoidance — the same withdrawal signature as the general shame spiral. **[Descriptive/qualitative, small samples — case studies and a 7-person qualitative study; not generalisable statistical findings, and RSD itself is explicitly not a formal diagnosis.]**

**Relevance to Hold:** consistent with the ADHD-community-informed positioning already documented (Hold is diagnosis-agnostic but partly informed by ADHD/PMDD/chronic-illness communities, per `07-business/06-business-strategy.md`'s marketing positioning) — RSD's withdrawal pattern is a plausible mechanism behind some users' communication freeze.

## Denial as coping / self-esteem and identity in chronic illness

- O'Donnell et al. (2022), *British Journal of Health Psychology* — internalized/anticipated stigma positively associated with illness self-concept (preoccupation with illness as part of identity) in concealable chronic illnesses. **[Correlational — cross-sectional survey association.]**

## Colour-mood research

- Jonauskaite & Mohr systematic review, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* — 132 peer-reviewed studies, 42,266 participants, 64 countries, 1895–2022. Colour-emotion associations are driven mainly by lightness and chroma, not hue — lighter/higher-chroma = happier, darker/lower-chroma = sadder. **[Correlational — large cross-cultural survey synthesis, not an experimental manipulation of colour exposure and mood.]**

**Relevance to Hold:** relevant context for the existing golden-hour Taking Time palette and the fresh/normal-state palette contrast already decided in `08-decisions/01-decision-log.md` (2026-07) — lightness/chroma, not hue choice alone, is the more evidenced lever for the emotional tone a palette carries.

## Shame Resilience Theory

- Brené Brown's Shame Resilience Theory (2006, grounded theory; since quantitatively tested) — four elements: acknowledging personal vulnerability to shame, critical awareness, reaching out, and "speaking the shame." **[Theoretical origin (grounded theory, qualitative); later quantitative testing exists but isn't detailed in the source compilation.]**

## Self-compassion interventions

The Self-Compassion Break (naming the feeling, common humanity, self-kindness) referenced in Hold's Transition screen design has a real lineage, not one collapsed reference — cited here as theory → practice → clinical validation, each for its own purpose:

- **Foundational theory** (where the three components — mindfulness, common humanity, self-kindness — were first academically established): Neff, K. D. (2003a). The development and validation of a scale to measure self-compassion. *Self and Identity*, 2, 223–250. **[Correlational/psychometric — scale-development and validation study.]** Neff, K. D. (2003b). Self-compassion: An alternative conceptualization of a healthy attitude toward oneself. *Self and Identity*, 2, 85–102. **[Theoretical — conceptual paper.]** Use these when explaining the underlying concept of self-compassion itself.
- **Practical evolution** (the theory becoming the actual named exercise, not a standalone journal paper — a practitioner/teaching source): Neff, K. D., & Germer, C. K. *The Mindful Self-Compassion Workbook*. New York, NY: Guilford Press. Also documented at self-compassion.org, Neff's own published teaching materials. **[Practitioner/teaching material — not a peer-reviewed empirical study; cite as the source of what the exercise is and where it comes from, not as evidence it works.]** Use this when describing what the Self-Compassion Break exercise is or where it comes from.
- **Clinical validation** (the evidence that practicing it actually works): Neff, K. D., & Germer, C. K. (2013). A pilot study and randomized controlled trial of the Mindful Self-Compassion program. *Journal of Clinical Psychology*, 69(1), 28–44. **[Causal — RCT, high confidence.]** Use this when making any claim that practicing the exercise is effective.

Separately, health-professions-specific application: Neff, K. D., Knox, M. C., Long, P., & Gregory, K. (2020). Caring for others without losing yourself: An adaptation of the Mindful Self-Compassion program for healthcare communities. *Journal of Clinical Psychology*, 76(9), 1543–1562. — the health-professions-specific study referenced in the original compilation but not fully detailed there. **[Causal — programme-adaptation/intervention study.] Confidence caveat, not fully settled:** this is a good-faith match based on subject relevance (health professions + Mindful Self-Compassion), not a certain one — the original reference was too vague to confirm with full confidence this is the exact paper intended. If this citation matters for something published-facing on the Research page, it's worth a final confirmation rather than being treated as settled.

**Citation note (resolved 2026-08-11):** both citations added via direct web search against the real papers, not reconstructed from memory — the first with high confidence, the second (health-professions study) carrying the caveat above.

**Relevance to Hold:** a plausible structural model (name the feeling, common humanity, self-kindness) for Hold's brief acknowledgment copy, consistent with the existing "gentle, short, genuine statements that validate" voice principle in `02-research/06-guilt-spiral-and-supportive-language.md` — not the origin of that principle, which predates this specific citation.

## Autonomy support / self-determination theory in health contexts

- Ng, J. Y. Y., Ntoumanis, N., Thøgersen-Ntoumani, C., Deci, E. L., Ryan, R. M., Duda, J. L., & Williams, G. C. (2012). Self-Determination Theory Applied to Health Contexts: A Meta-Analysis. *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, 7(4), 325–340. DOI: 10.1177/1745691612447309. — 184 independent datasets; practitioner support for patient autonomy correlates with patients' psychological need satisfaction, which in turn correlates with better mental and physical health outcomes, effect sizes up to 0.67 in some relationships. **[Correlational — not an intervention study.]**
- Ntoumanis, N., Ng, J. Y. Y., Prestwich, A., Quested, E., Hancox, J. E., Thøgersen-Ntoumani, C., Deci, E. L., Ryan, R. M., Lonsdale, C., & Williams, G. C. (2021). A meta-analysis of self-determination theory-informed intervention studies in the health domain. *Health Psychology Review*, 15(2), 214–244. — 73 experimental SDT-informed intervention studies; small-to-medium, heterogeneous effects on motivation and health behaviour. **[Causal — intervention-study meta-analysis, but the source itself describes the effects as modest and heterogeneous; do not overstate.]**

**Citation note (2026-08-19):** both papers independently verified against real search results (correct authors, journal, year, DOI, and topic) before being added here, not transcribed from a secondhand summary on trust. Note the second paper's lead author is Ntoumanis, not Williams — Williams (G. C. Williams) is a co-author, listed last per that field's convention for the senior/corresponding author, not the first author; cite as Ntoumanis et al., not Williams et al., if referenced elsewhere.

**Relevance to Hold:** supports the app's own "invitation, not obligation" register — the autonomy-supportive framing this literature studies (offering rather than prescribing) is the same instinct behind Hold's statements-over-questions, no-pressure voice rules.

## Music as emotion regulation

- Saarikallio, S. — body of work on music as an active emotion-regulation and coping strategy, spanning adolescence to late adulthood; repeatedly cited across the music-psychology literature. **[Theoretical/descriptive, drawing on both qualitative and quantitative studies across multiple papers — treat as a well-established framework, not one single citable effect size.]**
- Music and physiological stress recovery (cortisol, heart rate) — **genuinely contested, not settled.** At least two meta-analyses reach different conclusions: one finds no significant cumulative effect on physiological stress markers, another finds significant decreases in physiological arousal. **[Correlational/experimental, mixed across the literature — do not present either finding as the settled answer.]**
- Music as a "social surrogate" reducing loneliness — real experimental and qualitative support for music-listening reducing felt loneliness. **[Mixed experimental + qualitative support.]**

**Relevance to Hold:** if a future music/playlist feature is built (see `08-decisions/04-open-questions.md`), the emotion-regulation and loneliness findings support general music-listening as a plausible feature category; the contested physiological-stress-recovery finding should not be used as a specific claimed benefit.

## Sound-frequency / somatic nervous-system claims — flagged as practitioner-driven, not peer-reviewed consensus

Claims that specific sound frequencies (e.g. particular Hz ranges) directly affect the vagus nerve or autonomic nervous system are sourced mainly from sound-therapy practitioner/training organisations — e.g. the British Academy of Sound Therapy (BAST), London/Monmouth (confirmed as a real training organisation, founded 2000; not a peer-reviewed research body) — not from peer-reviewed consensus. **[Practitioner-driven — keep flagged as contested and distinct from the general music-listening research above; do not bundle the two categories together in copy or specs.]**

**Relevance to Hold:** if sound/frequency content is ever considered, it needs its own, separately-flagged confidence level from general music-listening content — the evidence bases are not equivalent.

## Chronic-illness-specific therapeutic intervention effect sizes — NOT evidence for passive app features

- Self-management interventions for depression in chronic physical disease — pooled effect on depression ≈ −0.47 (under 6 months) and ≈ −0.53 (6+ months) versus control. **[Causal — meta-analysis of intervention trials; medium-to-large effect.]**
- CBT for depression in multiple sclerosis specifically (15 trials, n = 1,508) — medium effect for depression (Hedges' g = 0.59), small effect for anxiety (g = 0.38). **[Causal — meta-analysis, independently verified 2026-08-19 against the real source.]**
- ACT (Acceptance and Commitment Therapy) for chronic pain — effect sizes clustering around d = 0.33–0.58 across trials on pain-related interference. **[Causal — meta-analytic range across trials.]**
- Telephone-delivered CBT for physical health outcomes in chronic illness — smaller effect, d = 0.225. **[Causal, smaller magnitude than the in-person interventions above.]**

**Explicit caveat — do not let these numbers imply an app notification would produce comparable effects.** Every intervention above is a structured therapeutic programme (weekly CBT sessions, guided self-management with skills training, delivered by a practitioner or a validated structured programme) — not a passive app feature. These are evidence for active, skills-based support, a genuinely different product category from what Hold currently is (a communication-facilitation tool, not a therapeutic intervention). Do not cite these effect sizes in support of any in-app nudge, reminder, or notification feature.

**Relevance to Hold:** supports keeping Hold positioned as communication support, not therapy — and is a caution against scope creep into claiming therapeutic-grade outcomes for lightweight app features.
