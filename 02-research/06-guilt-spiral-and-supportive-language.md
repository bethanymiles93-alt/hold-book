# Guilt-spiral and supportive language

This research directly informed Hold's voice and copy — not just the tone guidelines in `04-ux-content/02-voice-and-language.md`, but the specific decision to make every acknowledgment brief, validating, and free of praise or psychological commentary. It's surfaced to users in the app's own Research page (Settings), where Hold can honestly say it looked to the lived experience of people who deal with the guilt spiral when designing how it speaks.

## Shame and guilt are not the same thing — updated 2026-08-11

**This doc previously centred guilt alone. Shame is an equally or more central concern**, per the distinction established in shame/guilt research (Tangney et al. — see `02-research/07-extended-evidence-base.md`, "Shame vs. guilt," for the fuller research batch this was logged alongside; note that entry itself flags that a specific paper/year/DOI wasn't provided in the source compilation, so treat the attribution as a well-established framework rather than a single pinpointed study): **shame is a negative evaluation of the self as a whole** ("I am the kind of person who lets people down") **, while guilt is a negative evaluation of a specific behaviour** ("I didn't reply to that message"). The practical difference matters a great deal for a product like Hold: guilt can motivate repair (reaching back out), but shame tends to motivate withdrawal and hiding — the opposite of what Hold exists to help with. Language that reads as merely "about the guilt" but actually lands as a judgement of someone's character is the more dangerous failure mode, not a milder version of the same problem.

This reframes, not replaces, everything below: the existing guilt-spiral findings and voice rules still hold, but from here on this doc treats avoiding shame-inducing language (anything that implies something about who someone *is*) as at least as important as avoiding guilt-inducing language (anything that dwells on what someone *did or didn't do*).

## The core finding

Across accounts from people living with chronic illness and the guilt spiral around not replying, and from guidance on how to support them, one pattern is consistent: **short, genuine statements that validate the experience are more supportive than lengthy reassurances — and permission without pressure or commentary is what actually helps.**

Notably, the people actually living with this do not describe wanting to be praised for replying, or to have their guilt named back at them. Being told "well done" for sending a message, or being told an app "helps your guilt spiral," risks reframing basic communication as an achievement and explaining a person to themselves — both of which add pressure rather than relieving it.

## What this means for Hold's voice, everywhere

- Gentle, short, genuine statements that validate.
- Permission without pressure, and without commentary.
- Never praise the person for a basic act of communication ("well done" for sending a message).
- Never name or narrate the person's psychology back to them ("this helps your guilt spiral").
- **Never imply something about who someone IS, not just what they did or didn't do.** "You've been neglecting people" is a shame statement — it names a character trait ("you are neglectful"), not a behaviour. Even softer-sounding versions of this pattern count: "you've fallen behind on your circles," "you keep going quiet on people" — anything phrased as a standing trait or pattern of the person, rather than a single, neutral fact about time passing, risks landing as shame rather than guilt. Compare: "It's been 3 weeks" (neutral fact) vs. "You've let 3 weeks go by without checking in" (edges toward a judgement of character) — the first is safe under this rule, the second is not, independent of tone or politeness.
- This applies throughout the whole experience — welcome and first-launch copy, temporary/placeholder wording, transition screens, status labels, and every prompt — not only the moments that felt emotionally weighty when written.

## Sources

- ScienceInsights, "What to Say When Someone Isn't Feeling Well: Phrases That Help" — short, genuine, validating statements outperform lengthy reassurance; "I'm here if you want to talk, and totally fine if you don't" gives permission to lean in or protect energy, both without guilt.
- Hartzell Counseling, "How to Cope With Guilt Over Chronic Illness" — on the guilt-to-despair spiral and the role of self-permission in interrupting it.
- Further Food, "Guilt & Chronic Illness: How to Deal (or Help Someone Dealing)" — reassurance that you understand, and don't feel affected, lifts the load.
- Diary of an Introvert, "Texting Anxiety" — pre-written, gentle auto-messages ("I'm in recharge mode today") shift the mindset from guilt to self-respect, and let people know you're not ignoring them.
- Axial Spondyloarthritis / "My Chronic Struggles With Keeping Up With Replies" — first-person account of the reply guilt spiral and the value of an agreed low-effort signal for needing more time.

These are lived-experience and support-guidance sources, not clinical studies — used to shape tone and respect, not to make health claims. Hold remains diagnosis-agnostic and does not present itself as treatment.

The shame vs. guilt distinction above draws on academic (not lived-experience) research, logged separately in `02-research/07-extended-evidence-base.md` alongside the rest of the 2026-08-11 research batch — see that file for the correlational/theoretical tagging convention used for academic sources.
